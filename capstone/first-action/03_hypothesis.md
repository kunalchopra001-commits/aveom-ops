# 03 — Hypothesis

*My capstone hypothesis in my own words, with a number and one kill condition.*

---

## Hypothesis

Most of the work of turning shift records into paid hours is routine and a plain AI model
already does it as well as a person, faster. A minority of lines are disputed — the records
disagree — and settling those depends on things nobody wrote down, which the model cannot
retrieve and, worse, cannot tell it is missing. So the gain does not come from having an AI
help with everything. It comes from **knowing which lines are which**. I expect a system
that sends routine lines straight to the model and disputed lines to the person to settle
**at least 90% of lines correctly while cutting the person's time on the task by at least
half**, and I expect that a system where the person and the model work on *every* line
together will be **slower than either of them alone** and no more accurate.

## Kill condition

If, on the **disputed** lines, the bare model matches or beats the person, then the
unwritten knowledge I am claiming to be decisive is not decisive, the routing idea has
nothing to route, and the hypothesis is dead. I change the task rather than defend it.

## Second kill condition

If fewer than **30%** of lines turn out to be disputed, there is not enough of the hard
case to matter, and the honest conclusion is "automate it and stop" rather than anything
about collaboration.

---

## Why it is framed as routing, not assistance

A meta-analysis of 106 studies (Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone, *Nature Human Behaviour*,
2024) found human–AI combinations performed **worse** than the better of human-alone or
AI-alone (g = −0.23). The split: when humans beat the AI at the task, combining helped
(g = +0.46); when the AI beat humans, combining hurt (g = −0.54). Only three of the 106
studies allocated subtasks by which side was better at what. That is the gap this capstone
aims at, and this weekend measures which side of the line my task sits on.
